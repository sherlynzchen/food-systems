Chapter I — The Abundance Paradox

The world grows enough
to feed everyone.
So why doesn't it?

Every year, humanity produces over 9,800 trillion calories — enough for every person alive. Yet a third never becomes food. Follow the flow from field to fate.

Total production
9,800T
Trillion kcal produced globally per year — enough for 10 billion people at 2,500 kcal/day.
Lost or wasted
~30%
Of all food produced is lost post-harvest, in transit, at retail, or in homes. The largest single addressable intervention.
Used as animal feed
31%
Of global crop calories are fed to livestock, returning only 15–20% as meat and dairy — a caloric inefficiency at scale.

Sources: FAO Food Balance Sheets 2022 · WRAP Global Food Waste Assessment · IFPRI · Data illustrative based on published ranges.

Step 1 of 5
282 million people. One number.
In 2023, 282 million people across 59 countries faced acute food insecurity. Each dot is one million of them. Together they form a constellation of human suffering.
Step 2 of 5
Conflict: the largest single driver
135 million people — nearly half — live in countries where armed conflict is the primary cause. War destroys farms, supply chains, and markets. Hunger is a weapon.
Conflict
Step 3 of 5
Climate shocks hit the already vulnerable
77 million people face food insecurity primarily because of drought, flood, or extreme weather. They did least to cause climate change, yet bear the heaviest cost.
Climate shock
Step 4 of 5
Economic shocks erase the margins
93 million people tipped into food crisis by inflation, currency collapse, or income loss. A 20% rise in food prices is the difference between eating and not.
Economic crisis
Step 5 of 5
The same people. Multiple drivers.
More than 59% of all acutely food-insecure people face at least two simultaneous drivers. Solving one without the others is not enough.

Chapter VII — Structural Explanations

Agricultural employment
explains most food insecurity.
But not all of it.

Grouped by income level, a hidden pattern emerges: in low-income countries food insecurity closely tracks agricultural employment. As incomes rise, the two diverge sharply — pointing to inequality, conflict, and economic shocks as the real drivers.
